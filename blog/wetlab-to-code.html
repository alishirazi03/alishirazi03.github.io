From Wet Lab to Code: My Journey into Bioinformatics

How I transitioned from phenol-chloroform DNA extraction and TETRA-ARMS PCR to Python, Galaxy, and viral phylogenetics, and what I learned analyzing 100 SARS-CoV-2 genomes.

March 2026 20 min read Wet-Lab + Dry-Lab ATP8B1 & SARS-CoV-2

The Pipette and the Terminal

I still remember the first time I successfully amplified our target ATP8B1 gene fragment in the Translational Genomics Lab at COMSATS University Islamabad. After hours of optimizing thermal cycling profiles, adjusting annealing temperatures, and troubleshooting non-specific bands, crisp amplicons finally resolved under UV transillumination. Seeing that exact band pattern on the agarose gel felt like a genuine victory: coaxing genetic information out of whole blood samples using precise reagent volumes, organic extraction, and allele-specific primers.

That was my foundation as an undergraduate Biosciences student: a world of micropipettes, phenol-chloroform extractions, PCR tubes, and agarose gels. For my 12-credit final year thesis (awarded Grade A), I investigated the association of the ATP8B1 gene variant rs766737165 (T>A) with gallstone disease (cholelithiasis) across 104 clinical participants (75 confirmed patients and 29 healthy controls) in the Pakistani population. Spending months at the bench designing TETRA-ARMS PCR primers, running gels, and scoring genotypes taught me experimental discipline, quality control, and molecular genetics from first principles.

Yet as our cohort data came together, a larger question emerged.

The Realization: Scaling Beyond a Single Locus

As I compiled our genotyping dataset and ran Chi-square and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium models in R and Excel, a question kept nagging at me: How do we scale from querying a single candidate SNP to analyzing entire genomes and populations simultaneously?

Our experimental study revealed a monomorphic wild-type distribution for rs766737165 across our sampled cohort, establishing local baseline data and pointing toward the need for broader multi-exon screening. That finding made me think bigger: What about the rest of the 28 exons in ATP8B1? What about polygenic interactions across hepatobiliary transporters? And how did researchers assemble and track thousands of complete viral genomes within weeks during the COVID-19 pandemic?

The answer was not more pipettes alone. It was uniting wet-lab molecular biology with computational genomics.

Every modern genomic study I read, from population-scale variant discovery to real-time pathogen phylodynamics, relied on algorithmic pipelines. Wet-lab benchwork generates high-fidelity biological signal, while bioinformatics extracts patterns at genomic scale. Having spent four years building proficiency in molecular protocols, I decided to build the computational half of the bridge.

Taking the Leap into Computational Genomics

Alongside completing my undergraduate thesis, I enrolled in the Applied Bioinformatics Specialization from the University of California San Diego, complemented by courses in Python for Genomic Data Science and Genomic Technologies from Johns Hopkins University, and Clinical Data and Healthcare from Stanford University.

At first, terms like de Bruijn graphs, k-mer decomposition, N50 contiguity, and least-squares molecular clock dating felt like a foreign vocabulary. I was accustomed to thinking of DNA as a physical molecule precipitating in cold ethanol or migrating through an agarose matrix. Now I was looking at DNA as millions of 150 bp paired-end strings inside gigabyte-scale FASTQ files.

The unexpected advantage: Wet-lab training gives you biological intuition that pure software backgrounds rarely provide. Paired-end read orientation immediately made sense because I had designed forward and reverse PCR primers flanking a template. Open reading frame (ORF) prediction clicked immediately because of my coursework in molecular biology and biochemistry. The biology was familiar; I simply had to map it onto graph algorithms and command-line workflows.

The Transformation: Bench Protocol vs. Computational Pipeline

Comparing my two primary research projects side by side illustrates how experimental and computational workflows complement one another:

Wet-Lab Molecular Genetics

ATP8B1 Case-Control Study

  1. Sample Processing: Extract genomic DNA from 104 blood samples via phenol-chloroform protocol.
  2. Primer Engineering: Design inner/outer allele-specific primers in Primer3 and validate with BLAST.
  3. Assay Optimization: Tune MgCl2 and annealing temperature for TETRA-ARMS PCR.
  4. Gel Genotyping: Resolve amplicons via agarose gel electrophoresis and score bands under UV.
  5. Statistical Modeling: Compute Chi-square and Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium in R and Excel.

Outcome: Baseline population genotype distribution for SNP rs766737165 across 104 clinical subjects.

Computational Genomics

SARS-CoV-2 100-Genome Pipeline

  1. Data Ingestion: Fetch 2.5 million raw Illumina reads (SRR10971381) and 100 isolate genomes from NCBI.
  2. De Novo Assembly & QC: Reconstruct the 29,903 bp genome in SPAdes and benchmark in QUAST.
  3. Functional Annotation: Identify all 11 viral ORFs with Prokka and design diagnostic primers.
  4. Comparative Alignment: Align Spike variants in MUSCLE and 100 full genomes via ViralMSA.
  5. Phylodynamics & Pathways: Build ML trees in FastTree, date tMRCA in LSD2, and map BioCyc targets.

Outcome: Full viral assembly, evolutionary dating (Oct–Nov 2019), and guanylate kinase drug target identification.

Five Lessons I Wish I Knew Before Starting

1. Start with Workflows Before Worrying About Software Engineering

A common misconception among biology students is that you must spend years mastering computer science before running your first genomic analysis. Beginning with workflow engines like Galaxy allowed me to master the logic of k-mer assembly, quality metrics, and substitution models first, and then transition smoothly into Python, R, and Bash scripting.

2. Wet-Lab Knowledge Is a Major Analytical Advantage

When designing diagnostic RT-PCR primers for the SARS-CoV-2 Nucleocapsid (N) gene or evaluating sequencing artifacts, my bench experience with melting temperatures ($T_m$), GC clamps, primer dimers, and DNA extraction contaminants paid immediate dividends. Understanding how biological samples are prepared makes it much easier to spot technical noise in sequencing data.

3. Troubleshooting Failed PCRs Transfers Directly to Debugging Code

Troubleshooting a failed TETRA-ARMS PCR reaction and debugging a broken bioinformatics pipeline rely on the exact same scientific method: isolate variables, inspect input quality, verify parameter flags, and test against positive controls.

4. Computational Models Reveal Evolutionary Time

Running FastTree and LSD2 across 100 dated viral isolates to estimate the pandemic's time to most recent common ancestor (tMRCA: October–November 2019, $R^2 = 0.96$) showed me how sequence divergence encodes historical timelines. Aligning the Spike receptor-binding domain (RBD) in MUSCLE and observing the 6 key amino acid substitutions relative to SARS-CoV-1 made molecular evolution visible at single-residue resolution.

5. Negative Wet-Lab Results and Computational Predictions Reinforce Each Other

In my thesis, observing a monomorphic genotype distribution at a single candidate SNP highlighted why whole-exome and bioinformatic pre-screening are so valuable before committing wet-lab reagents. Conversely, identifying guanylate kinase (EC 2.7.4.8) as an antiviral metabolic chokepoint in BioCyc underscored that computational target discovery ultimately requires wet-lab enzymatic and cellular validation.

Advice for Biosciences Students Making the Transition

  • Anchor new algorithms to familiar wet-lab concepts: Relate paired-end read insert sizes to PCR amplicons, and relate de Bruijn graph contigs to Sanger sequencing chromatograms.
  • Learn on real biological datasets: Working on actual clinical cohorts or pandemic sequencing accessions (such as SRR10971381) keeps the algorithmic math grounded in biological discovery.
  • Document everything in public repositories: Publishing reproducible workflows, raw outputs, and technical write-ups on GitHub demonstrates your capabilities far more effectively than listing tool names on a CV alone.
  • Treat wet-lab and dry-lab as a single discipline: You are not leaving the laboratory behind; you are expanding the scale of questions you can ask.

Current Milestones & Next Steps

Since beginning this transition from bench protocols to computational workflows, I have:

  • Completed my B.S. in Biosciences at COMSATS University Islamabad (134 credit hours; 12-credit thesis on ATP8B1 in 104 participants awarded Grade A).
  • Built and documented an end-to-end SARS-CoV-2 genomic analysis pipeline across 100 viral genomes and de novo reference assembly (29,903 bp).
  • Completed specialized coursework in Applied Bioinformatics (UC San Diego), Python for Genomic Data Science & Genomic Technologies (Johns Hopkins), and Clinical Data / Healthcare (Stanford).
  • Joined the International Society for Computational Biology (ISCB) as a Student Member (ID: 46230) and achieved an IELTS Academic Overall Band 7.5 (CEFR Level C1).

I am currently preparing applications for European Master's programs in Bioinformatics, Computational Biology, and Molecular Biosciences (2027 intake), with the goal of conducting integrative research that combines experimental molecular genetics with high-throughput computational analysis.

Assembling a Pandemic: My First Genome Assembly

Walking through the process of reconstructing the complete 29,903 bp SARS-CoV-2 viral genome from 2.5 million short Illumina DNA reads using SPAdes, QUAST, and Galaxy.

March 2026 18 min read SRA: SRR10971381 SPAdes · QUAST · BLAST
NCBI SRA Dataset SRR10971381
Raw Input Reads 2,512,682 (150 bp PE)
Largest Contig / N50 29,903 bp
Mean Depth / GC% 193.5x · 37.97%

The Problem: 2.5 Million Pieces of a Viral Puzzle

Imagine I dumped a 30,000-piece jigsaw puzzle on your table, but there is a catch: I first shredded each piece into dozens of tiny fragments, mixed them all together, and then asked you to reconstruct the original image. On top of that, some fragments are damaged, some are duplicates, and about 10% do not even belong to this puzzle because they are contamination from other sources.

Welcome to genome assembly.

When I downloaded dataset SRR10971381 from NCBI (raw sequencing data from a COVID-19 patient in Wuhan, China, collected in early January 2020), I was not getting a neat, finished SARS-CoV-2 genome sequence. I was getting 2,512,682 short DNA reads, each only 150 base pairs long. The complete viral genome is 29,903 nucleotides. That is roughly 200 times more coverage than needed, but broken into tiny, overlapping fragments that had to be computationally stitched back together.

This is the story of learning genome assembly, one of the most foundational skills in bioinformatics, by reconstructing the genome of the virus that caused a global pandemic.

What I Knew (and Didn't Know)

What My Wet-Lab Background Taught Me

From my undergraduate molecular genetics work on the ATP8B1 gene, I understood:

  • DNA sequencing basics: Sanger sequencing produces clean, relatively long reads (~800 bp).
  • PCR amplicons: You amplify a targeted region and inspect a single readable chromatogram.
  • The core workflow: Extracted DNA → thermal cycler / sequencer → sequence output.

I was used to seeing clean, ordered sequence outputs that looked like this:

ATCGATCGATCGATCG...

What I Didn't Understand Yet

Next-generation short-read sequencing (Illumina) operates on a different scale altogether:

  • Millions of short reads (150 bp) instead of a single continuous read.
  • Paired-end sequencing (reads sequenced from both ends of sheared DNA fragments).
  • Raw FASTQ files packed with ASCII-encoded Phred quality scores.
  • No inherent read ordering: determining their order is the entire job of the assembler.
  • High computational complexity that quickly overwhelms standard desktop tools.

When I first opened the SRR10971381 dataset, I expected something human-readable. What I got was a 1.2 GB file containing blocks like this repeated 2.5 million times: 

@SRR10971381.1
NGCGGATTTCGTCACCCACGTGGCGGATGTCATAGGTTATAATAATATTCGTATGGCGGCG
+
#8ACCGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGG

At first glance, the @ headers, + separators, and ASCII quality strings looked like a completely different language. My goal was to turn this raw read file into a verified, complete viral genome.

The Jigsaw Puzzle Analogy (And Where It Breaks Down)

Before diving into the graph algorithm, it helps to compare genome assembly directly with a traditional jigsaw puzzle:

Traditional Jigsaw Puzzle

  • Pieces: Each piece has a distinct physical shape and visual pattern.
  • Borders: Flat edge pieces let you frame the boundary first.
  • Reference Image: You have the box cover as a visual guide.
  • Strategy: Group by color and connect unambiguous interlocking edges.
  • End Result: One complete picture.

Genome Assembly Puzzle

  • Pieces: Short DNA reads (150 bp each).
  • Borders: No obvious start or end markers across the fragmented reads.
  • Reference Image: Unknown in de novo assembly (discovering it is the objective).
  • Strategy: Identify exact and near-exact k-mer prefix/suffix overlaps.
  • End Result: Continuous assembled sequences (contigs).

Here is why de novo assembly is substantially harder than a standard puzzle:

Challenge 1: Genomic Repeats
Imagine a puzzle with large patches of identical blue sky. DNA genomes contain repetitive sequences where the exact same 100 bp motif appears in multiple loci. An assembler must determine whether two reads matching that sequence originate from the same locus or separate regions.

Challenge 2: No Clear Edge Pieces
Even though viral genomes have defined 5' and 3' ends, individual sheared reads do not carry "border" tags, and coverage often tapers off near the termini.

Challenge 3: Sequencing Errors and Host Contamination
Some reads contain base-calling errors, while others originate from host human transcripts or commensal bacteria co-extracted from the clinical swab. The pipeline must filter and separate these from true viral reads.

Challenge 4: High Coverage Depth (~200x)
Instead of one copy of each puzzle piece, deep sequencing yields roughly 200 overlapping copies of every genomic region. The assembler must distinguish high-depth duplicates from genuine genomic repeats.

Challenge 5: Combinatorial Scale
Inspecting 2.5 million fragments manually is impossible. We rely on graph algorithms that systematically decompose reads and traverse optimal paths.

My First Attempt: Understanding the Input Data

I ran my initial workflow on Galaxy, a web-based bioinformatics workbench that wraps command-line genomics tools into reproducible pipelines.

Step 1: Decoding the FASTQ Format

Each four-line record in a FASTQ file represents a single sequencing read:

@SRR10971381.1 1 length=150
NGCGGATTTCGTCACCCACGTGGCGGATGTCATAGGTTATAATAATATTCGTATGGCGGCG
+
#8ACCGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGGG
  • Line 1 (@): Read identifier and metadata (read #1, length 150 bp).
  • Line 2: Nucleotide sequence (A, T, C, G, and N for ambiguous base calls).
  • Line 3 (+): Delimiter line.
  • Line 4: ASCII-encoded Phred quality scores (where J indicates high confidence with error probability ~0.001%, and # indicates an uncalled or low-confidence base corresponding to the initial N).
Lesson 1: Raw sequencing data is heterogeneous in quality. Error correction before or during graph construction is essential so low-quality bases do not create false branches.

Step 2: Leveraging Paired-End Sequencing

The SRA accession contained two separate files:

  • SRR10971381_1.fastq (forward reads)
  • SRR10971381_2.fastq (reverse reads)

In paired-end Illumina sequencing:

  1. Viral RNA is reverse-transcribed and sheared into fragments of known approximate insert size (e.g., ~500 bp): [----- ~500 bp fragment -----]
  2. Both ends of each fragment are sequenced inward:
    • Forward read: ATCGAT... (150 bp from the 5' end)
    • Reverse read: ...GCTAGC (150 bp from the 3' end)

Because the assembler knows that read pair .1 and .2 originated from the same physical DNA molecule separated by a known insert distance, it can use paired-end distance constraints to bridge repetitive regions.

Lesson 2: Paired-end read orientation and insert-size distribution provide long-range spatial constraints that resolve graph ambiguities.

The Assembly Algorithm: De Bruijn Graphs

Before executing SPAdes, I studied its underlying algorithmic framework: de Bruijn graphs.

Why the Naive Pairwise Overlap Approach Fails

A naive overlap-layout-consensus approach compares every read against every other read. With $2.5 \times 10^6$ reads, pairwise all-versus-all alignment requires $(2.5 \times 10^6)^2 = 6.25 \times 10^{12}$ (6.25 trillion) comparisons, which is computationally prohibitive.

How De Bruijn Graphs Solve the Scaling Bottleneck

Instead of aligning full reads pairwise, SPAdes decomposes each read into overlapping substrings of length $k$, called k-mers. For example, with $k = 25$, a 26 bp sequence: 

ATCGATCGATCGATCGATCGATCG (26 bp)

yields two consecutive 25-mers:

ATCGATCGATCGATCGATCGATC  (positions 1-25)
 TCGATCGATCGATCGATCGATCG (positions 2-26)

Across all 2.5 million reads, identical k-mers collapse into single graph vertices:

  1. Nodes (Vertices): Each unique k-mer (or $(k-1)$-mer) represents a node.
  2. Directed Edges: A directed edge connects k-mer $A$ to k-mer $B$ if the $(k-1)$ suffix of $A$ matches the $(k-1)$ prefix of $B$.
  3. Eulerian Walk: Reconstructing the genome reduces to finding a continuous walk through the graph.
Lesson 3: Transforming pairwise sequence alignment ($O(n^2)$) into k-mer hashing and graph traversal reduces assembly to near-linear complexity.

Running the SPAdes Assembler

Inside Galaxy, I configured the SPAdes pipeline as follows:

  1. Tool: SPAdes genome assembler
  2. Forward Reads: SRR10971381_1.fastq
  3. Reverse Reads: SRR10971381_2.fastq
  4. Multi-k-mer Iteration: k = 21, 33, 55
  5. Execution Time: ~45 minutes

Under the Hood of the SPAdes Pipeline

  • Step 1 - Bayesian Error Correction (BayesHammer): Uses k-mer coverage frequencies (~200x depth) to identify and correct singleton sequencing errors prior to assembly.
  • Step 2 - Iterative Multi-k-mer Graph Construction: Builds graphs sequentially across $k = 21, 33, 55$. Smaller k-mers maintain connectivity in lower-coverage regions, while larger k-mers span short repeats.
  • Step 3 - Graph Simplification: Prunes low-coverage dead-end tips (sequencing errors at read termini), Chimeric edges, and bubbles (SNPs or PCR artifacts).
  • Step 4 - Repeat Resolution (exSPAnder): Applies paired-end insert distances to trace the true genomic path through repeat junctions.
  • Step 5 - Contig Output: Emits assembled contiguous sequences in FASTA format (contigs.fasta).

Seeing NODE_1_length_29903_cov_193.456 immediately answered the core diagnostic questions:

  • Did we recover the full viral genome? Yes: 29,903 bp matches the canonical SARS-CoV-2 reference length in a single contiguous sequence.
  • What was the sequencing depth? 193.5x mean k-mer coverage across the viral genome.
  • What are the secondary short nodes (e.g., 184 bp at 12.7x coverage)? Low-coverage background fragments from host RNA or bacterial flora co-extracted in the clinical specimen.

Evaluating Assembly Contiguity with QUAST

To benchmark the assembly formally, I ran QUAST (Quality Assessment Tool for Genome Assemblies):

Number of contigs : 3
Total length      : 30,121 bp
Largest contig    : 29,903 bp
N50               : 29,903 bp
GC%               : 37.97%

Understanding the N50 Statistic

If you sort all assembled contigs from longest to shortest and sum their lengths until you reach 50% of the total assembly length, N50 is the length of the contig at that 50% threshold.

  • Toy Example: Given contigs of 100 bp, 80 bp, 50 bp, and 30 bp (total = 260 bp; 50% threshold = 130 bp), summing $100 + 80 = 180\text{ bp}$ crosses 130 bp at the 80 bp contig, so $\text{N50} = 80\text{ bp}$.
  • Our Assembly: $\text{N50} = 29,903\text{ bp}$, confirming that a single full-length contig accounts for over 99% of the total assembled sequence.
Lesson 4: N50 (and reference-based NG50) quantifies contiguity, while BLAST alignment and GC content verify biological accuracy.

Biological Validation with NCBI BLAST

1. Verification Against Reference SARS-CoV-2

Aligning NODE_1 against NCBI nt (viral sequences) returned:

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 isolate Wuhan-Hu-1
Query Cover      : 100%
Percent Identity : 99.99%

2. Identifying the Closest Pre-Pandemic Relative

Filtering out SARS-CoV-2 entries to query pre-2020 coronaviruses returned:

Bat coronavirus RaTG13
Query Cover      : 98%
Percent Identity : 96.1%

This reproduced the landmark evolutionary finding from early 2020: SARS-CoV-2 shares 96.1% nucleotide identity with the bat coronavirus RaTG13, confirming its natural zoonotic origin.

3. GC Content Sanity Check

Coronaviruses are AT-rich with an expected GC content of ~38%. Our assembled genome measured 37.97% GC, ruling out chimeric bacterial misassembly.

Common Assembly Failure Modes & Troubleshooting

Failure Mode Symptom in Output Mitigation Strategy
Low Sequencing Coverage (<15x) Fragmented assembly; low N50; many short contigs Increase sequencing depth or include smaller k-mer sizes
Host / Bacterial Contamination Extra short contigs with divergent coverage and GC% Filter contigs by coverage depth (~193x vs 12x) and BLAST taxid
Long Genomic Repeats (>Read Length) Collapsed repeats or broken contig boundaries Use paired-end libraries with larger insert sizes or long reads (Nanopore/PacBio)
High Base-Call Error Rate Massive graph branching and dead-end tips Trim adapters/low-Q bases (FastQC/Trimmomatic) and run BayesHammer

De Novo vs. Reference-Guided Assembly

  • De Novo Assembly (Used Here): Reconstructs the sequence from scratch using k-mer overlaps alone. Computationally intensive, but essential when characterizing a novel pathogen or uncatalogued structural rearrangements without reference bias.
  • Reference-Guided Mapping: Aligns reads directly to an existing reference genome (e.g., Wuhan-Hu-1) using tools like BWA or Bowtie2. Faster and ideal for routine variant calling across large cohorts, which I subsequently applied when aligning 100 SARS-CoV-2 isolates.

Reproduce This Workflow Yourself

You can replicate this exact assembly in under two hours using free public infrastructure:

  1. Fetch Raw Reads: Log in to usegalaxy.org and import accession SRR10971381 from NCBI SRA using Faster Download and Extract Reads in FASTQ.
  2. Assemble with SPAdes: Select SPAdes, input the forward (_1) and reverse (_2) paired-end FASTQ files, keep multi-k-mer defaults (21, 33, 55), and execute.
  3. Benchmark with QUAST: Run QUAST on the resulting contigs.fasta file to inspect N50, contig count, and GC percentage.
  4. Validate with BLASTn: Query the 29,903 bp contig against NCBI nt to verify 99.99% identity with Wuhan-Hu-1 and 96.1% identity with RaTG13.
